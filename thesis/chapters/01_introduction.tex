Since the publication of the Einstein's field equations, the interest for a new type of celestial object: the a Black hole (BH). This object receives its name from the fact that it does not emit apparent radiation; what we know about them come from the gravitational interaction, such as the merge event detected in 2016 through the signal GW150914 \cite{Abbott2016}. Going back to the first analytical solutions of these equations, the one corresponding to a spherically symmetric spacetime is the  Schwarzschild metric. This metric contains two singularities: one is inevitable, the other, the event horizon, is our case of study. The horizon is a hypersuface that acts as a membrane, preventing information from crossing between the two regions it separates. 

We have so far discussed only a static solution of the field equations, but in 1939 Oppenheimer and Snyder found a incredible result: the field equations can also predict a dynamic solution \cite{Oppenheimer-Snyder1939}. This solution is based on the simplest model of a star, a dusk star, which, under this approximation representing a dying star, they used to show how a collapsing star forms the membrane that hides what lies inside.  This dynamic solution was one of the problems that unleash new physics, giving birth to both black holes physics and gravitational waves (GW) physics. To determine how a BH forms and how a gravitational wave is produced, a new way of approaching the field equations become necessary, since numerical integration alone is not sufficient to solve them. This is precisely the purpose of numerical relativity: a field devoted to understanding and appropriately discretizing spacetime, in order to predict how matter evolves and how that evolution affects spacetime in turn \cite{baumgarte2021numerical}. 

In the development of the collapse, Oppenheimer and Snyder discuss two different metrics that somehow need to be connected. On one side we have the star, modeled here simply as dust, since the main focus is its connection with the outside. The interior is described by the Tolman metric. This metric is known as comoving, meaning that its coordinates move together with the matter, and in particular with the boundary hypersurface separating the interior of the star from the outer vacuum. Initially, it was conceived as a model to describe the universe as a non-homogeneous dust distribution with spherical symmetry about some center. Although this was its original purpose, to capture the effects of non-uniformity, in contrast with the Friedmann-Lemaître-Robertson-Walker (FLRW) metric, which is completely uniform in all directions, it also serves to describe the interior of a star, thanks to the symmetries involved. In Einstein's equations, scale does not matter: the same formalism can describe a star or the entire universe. /cite of tolman article/

Speaking of dust, it is a point that needs to be addressed. What is meant by dust in this context? Dust describes particles with mass that have little or no interaction with each other, and therefore exert no pressure. They behave essentially as individual particles interacting with spacetime independently.

What Oppenheimer and Snyder achieved was taking Tolman's cosmological solution, truncating it at an outer radius $R$, and matching it to the external vacuum Schwarzschild metric to model a collapsing star. By coupling this geometry with the simple energy-momentum tensor of dust, they obtained a set of equations that describe a purely dynamic process. Following Birkhoff's theorem /cita of birkhoff/, the exterior spacetime remains spherically symmetric, and by matching the Tolman and Schwarzschild metrics at the boundary, they explicitly derived the evolution of the collapsing hypersurface. These results demonstrate the inevitability of total collapse: once a star exhausts its core energy production, it is sustained only by its own gravity. The mass compresses indefinitely, and once the horizon forms, all outgoing radiation becomes infinitely redshifted as seen by an external observer. Ultimately, the interior becomes completely isolated from the outside world; from that same external viewpoint, time itself appears to freeze at the horizon, the two faces, vanishing light and frozen time, being simply different manifestations of the same underlying effect.

The success of the OS model illustrates both the power and the limitation of analytical solutions in general relativity: exact results are only accessible under strongly idealized assumptions. To study more realistic or more general collapse scenarios, one must instead turn to numerical methods. A computer allows one to relax these idealizations and introduce additional physical ingredients, for instance, a small amount of angular momentum, treated perturbatively around the spherically symmetric background; some electric charge; or even an additional field, motivated by alternative theories of gravity. Even within spherical symmetry, however, the matching between the interior and exterior metrics reduces the collapse to a system of ordinary differential equations governing the evolution of the collapsing hypersurface. Solving this system reliably, with adequate control over numerical error and stability, is itself a nontrivial problem, and it is precisely the one addressed in this work. % there is a reference to the work

The OS collapse shows not only the process in which a star could die, but also the way on how to put together two metrics, the connection is Initially just a tool, but it provides glance into the necessary conditions in which are now know as the Darmois-Israel formalism. These conditions originate to preserve the curvature and causal nature (check that) between the two regions, in other words, a smooth transition between the metrics in question. However, under these conditions there is room for a disruption, leading to the division of space into different parts: an interior, an exterior and the boundary between them corresponds to an induced metric and ca be interpreted as a hypersuface, a shell
